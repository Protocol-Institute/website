AI Kitcraft: A Hands-On AI Tooling Workshop

Workshop · SIGP4B (Protocols for Business)

Abstract

Most teams "adopt AI" by buying new, and mostly untested software, then wait for a productivity jump that never shows up in the numbers. That missing productivity is the signature of the stage we are actually in: the Kit phase. Every general-purpose technology — the steam engine, the Model T, the PC — passes through it: loose components people tinker with, repurpose, and master by doing, long before anyone builds the firm-scale machinery that turns tinkering into measured output. Farmers ran corn shellers and water pumps off jacked-up Model Ts for years before Ford watched them and built the tractor. The work that matters happens in undocumented local experiments, and the organizations that pull ahead are the ones that recognize the stage they are in and build a map to climb out of it. AI Kitcraft is the hands-on answer. Across the symposium's two workshop days — one presentation and four working sessions, plus asynchronous co-working on Discord — participants move from ad-hoc AI use to building their first Kit: a reusable bundle of workflows, disposable applications, and contexts a team can run repeatedly and reliably. Then they map a sustainable path to scale it. Every level is touched by hand: participants Play, build a Kit, and prepare to turn it into a Practice. No AI-engineering background is required — only regular chatbot use and a willingness to tinker. Each participant leaves with at least one working Kit they can re-run and a grasp of the five-level AI Capability Maturity Model.

Audience

The workshop is aimed at AI-curious and non-technical analysts and managers from all backgrounds — project managers, operations and deployment leads, and AI-curious newcomers. No AI-engineering background is required. Participants are required to (1) have used AI chatbots regularly (e.g. ChatGPT), (2) have installed Claude Code (Pro+) or OpenAI's Codex, (3) have an available GitHub account, and (4) bring one recurring task they want to build a Kit around. If oversubscribed, priority goes to applicants who arrive with a concrete recurring task and a working tooling setup.

What participants will take away

By the end, each participant has (1) at least one working Kit they built and can re-run, and (2) a working understanding of the five-level AI Capability Maturity Model — enough to locate their organization on it and plan the next level up. They also leave with the language and design principles of Kitcraft and a one-page sketch for turning their Kit into an organizational capability.

What participants will do

Participants build, trade, and demo. They will: set up an environment and assemble a context hub (goals, references, conventions the AI works from); deploy a first disposable application or wireframe; convert it into a reusable Kit that encodes the context, prompts, and steps for repeated use; participate in a "Kit trade," where a paired team builds a small improvement on another's Kit; and present in a closing show-and-tell. Products: at least one working, re-runnable Kit; its documented context/prompts/steps; and a short plan for converting the Kit into an organizational capability. Async, participants share works-in-progress, debug, and trade kits on Discord.

Notes

Maximum workshop participants: 10–15 (see flag below re: the symposium's stated 30–50 target) Format: One presentation and four working sessions across the two workshop days, plus async co-working on Discord (kit-building help, debugging, sharing what people make). This fits the symposium's "4–6 Zoom sessions of ~60–90 minutes" workshop format. Presentation — Fog of War Kit Building Amateur kit-making cultures map out unexplored terrains of technology. In the early stages, this exploration takes the form of a spectacle, with the focus on spreading the message and getting other people interested in the technology. Alongside the spectacle, tacit knowledge develops — undocumented, passed along through practice rather than print. This knowledge lives in workshops, club meetings, and newsletters, accumulating in the hands of practitioners long before any formal institution takes notice. In this session, we look at the history of amateur kit-making and the strategies these communities employed: from amateur radio operators carving out regulatory space for themselves, to phone phreaks creating the perfect setting for the makers of the desktop era of computing. Then we map these strategies onto working with AI, asking what today's tinkerers can borrow from a century of amateurs who turned play into infrastructure. Workshop — AI Kit Club Prior to the first working session, participants receive a "Kitcraft Kit" that guides their Kit development through the working sessions — language and principles for Kit design. During the workshop, participants are paired and co-create a Kit related to their common interests. Working Session 1 — Context Play: Set up your environment, assemble a basic context hub for your Kit — the small bundle of goals, references, and conventions the AI works from — and deploy your first disposable application or wireframe. Working Session 2 — Kitcraft I: Convert your disposable application into a reusable Kit: a workflow you, or a teammate, can run repeatedly and reliably. The Kit encodes the context, prompts, and steps for usage. Begins with a short clinic on the success patterns and failure modes of AI adoption, and ends with a Kit trade, where another pair builds a small improvement on your Kit. Working Session 3 — Kitcraft II: Continue hardening the Kit. As with Session 2, opens with a clinic on AI-adoption patterns and failure modes and closes with a Kit trade. Working Session 4 — Show and Tell: Kit demo day. Each participant shows the Kit they built and shares how they might convert it into an organizational capability. Example Kit outcome: Agent-Accessible Brand Kit — https://npc.here.now/zknationbrand/ Audience: project managers, operations and deployment leads, and AI-curious newcomers. Prerequisites: a laptop, access to any major AI assistant, and one recurring task to work on. Facilitated by the Protocols for Business SIG (part of the Protocol Institute). Foundations: the AI Capability Maturity Model (ai.protocolized.dev); Sachin Benny, AI, Tractors, and the Productivity Paradox (the kit thesis); and the 2026 Protocol Symposium, New Nature.
