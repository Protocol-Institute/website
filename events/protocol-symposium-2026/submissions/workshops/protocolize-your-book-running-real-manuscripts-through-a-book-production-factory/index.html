Protocolize Your Book: Running Real Manuscripts Through a Book Production Factory

Workshop · SIGPfB (Protocols for Business)

Abstract

Most "protocol" workshops stay abstract. This one is a live factory. Participants bring real manuscripts — a half-finished book, a pile of essays, a thread archive — and over four sessions we push them through an actual protocolized production pipeline: a transmittal handshake that generates a custom authoring template, a conformance "preflight" that negotiates messy human input into machine-readable structure, and a deterministic transform that yields print PDF and EPUB from one source. Along the way participants handle the underlying protocol concepts directly — typed interfaces, controlled vocabularies, conformance and negotiation, layered determinism, inheritable templates — and delegate drafting and checking to LLM agents to see why a human-legible protocol is also machine-legible. Everyone leaves with a typeset artifact and a felt answer to the real question: how much of a craft should be protocolized, and at what cost?

Audience

Writers, editors, designers, small-press and self-publishers, zine-makers, newsletter authors, and protocol-curious people who have a body of text they'd like to see become a real book — plus anyone interested in what it feels like to protocolize a creative craft. No coding, typesetting, or prior protocol-studies background required; the factory handles the mechanics, participants make the protocol decisions. The one hard requirement is bringing real material.

Selection criteria: (1) Has a real manuscript or text collection ready to bring (any size; rough is welcome) — the workshop runs on participants' own material. (2) Diversity of source material: a half-finished book, an essay pile, a thread archive, a zine, poetry — so the conformance and negotiation sessions surface a wide range of edge cases. (3) Able to attend all four sessions — the pipeline is cumulative. (4) Tiebreaker: people new to protocol studies and people from publishing/craft backgrounds, to widen the conversation beyond the usual technical audience.

What participants will take away

  • A typeset PDF and EPUB of their own material, produced through the factory.
  • A custom authoring template generated from their transmittal, reusable for future work.
  • A transferable mental model: their own craft re-seen as a protocol stack — typed interfaces, controlled vocabularies, conformance/negotiation, layered determinism, inheritable templates.
  • A decision rubric for what to protocolize and what to leave to craft judgment, applicable well beyond book production.
  • First-hand experience delegating creative-production work to LLM agents and an understanding of why it works — a protocol legible to people is legible to machines.
  • A felt, not just theoretical, answer to the symposium's question: where protocolization pays off, and where it leaks.

What participants will do

This is a hands-on lab, not a lecture. Concretely, participants will:

  • Fill a real transmittal form for their manuscript and receive a generated custom authoring template (Session 1).
  • Run their messy input through the preflight conformance detector, then make Keep/Strip/Convert decisions — individually and as a group reading real findings (Session 2).
  • Produce build artifacts: a print PDF and an EPUB from one conformant source, and inspect the format-independent intermediate (Session 3).
  • Direct an LLM agent to draft a missing section and propose preflight decisions, comparing its output against the same interface a human targets (Session 3).
  • Give a short lightning show-and-tell of their produced artifact plus one place the protocol helped and one place it fought them, feeding a group synthesis and a shared rubric (Session 4).

Tangible outputs: a completed transmittal, a custom template, a typeset PDF + EPUB, a set of conformance decisions, and a one-slide reflection per participant.

Organizers

Jenna Dixon

Jenna Dixon has built books and run digital operations independently since 1991 — web strategy, book design, and composition. She now runs jdbb studio, a "book-production factory with a human in the loop": the very pipeline this workshop puts participants inside. As past Executive Director of the Vermont Book Professionals Association she spent years convening editors, designers, and printers around the practical handoffs of publishing — which is exactly the coordination problem this workshop reframes as a protocol. Jenna taught graphic design and computing as adjunct faculty, giving her a track record of getting non-technical people productive with technical tools. A long-running Yak Collective member, Jenna participates in weekly study groups on governance and composable/distributed systems, so facilitating a hands-on, multi-session protocol exploration is a native mode. She holds an MPA (Development Economics) from MIIS and a BA cum laude from Dartmouth.
