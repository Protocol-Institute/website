SIG-MRG 2026-11-05

Participants: Mark Elder, Bárbara, vengi, Kei 🗝

Kei opened with a reflective exercise asking participants to recall one vivid and one dim memory and notice the qualitative differences, framed by Peirce's distinction between vividness and dimness. After a period of silent reading, discussion turned to whether habit is a form of memory, how it relates to association, and where memories/ideas "reside." The conversation situated Peirce within a series of prior readings (Levin, Sheldrake) on the location and nature of memory and ideas.

  • Vividness vs. dimness (opening exercise): Kei: A recent autumn hike felt vivid — crisp air, sharp multicolored foliage, distinct remembered moments — whereas repeated later hikes of the same trail felt dim, blurred, with fewer locatable moments. Mark: Tangible/lived memories vs. abstract/intellectual memories feel stored in different parts of the body. Remembering a difficult Voegelin book he read felt physically different from recalling walking through the Old Port in Montreal. He linked this to NLP's eye-position and sensory-modality cues (while noting the lack of scientific basis). vengi: A vivid memory of a child's birthday party in British Columbia where adults crossed a "veil" in the forest, wore red clown noses, spoke only gibberish, and played as children — vivid due to uniqueness and recency. Dim examples: uncertainty over whether a conversation already happened (or déjà vu), and a photo whose environment (a place connected to his mother's former partner) he couldn't recall because it was ephemeral and never "stabilized." Bárbara: Recurring vivid childhood fragments (playing with her sister) kept alive by repeated recall; contrasted with a recent, nebulous memory triggered by an email from someone who knew her from a bar she once worked at.
  • Peirce's framework on memory and ideas (Kei's framing): Peirce straddles materialism and immaterialism — offering a materialist account of how ideas and memories arise and interact, unlike pure Platonism or Sheldrake's largely immaterial fields. Association by resemblance and by contiguity; physical proximity of people produces shared memories. Memories/ideas can become transpersonal — forming a kind of field or separate entity that people inhabit but need not inhabit. Habit extends even to the "laws of nature" (e.g., the speed of light framed as a habit of matter). Kei cited a Peirce line from elsewhere: "matter is mind deadened by habit."
  • Is habit a memory? (central question): vengi: Yes — habit and memory codetermine each other; habits can form that lead to association, and habits also change over time. Kei: There may be different types of memory. Human memory implies self-reflexivity (something witnessing the memory), whereas habit tends toward diminished self-reflexivity and reduced optionality (invoking the earlier Leroi-Gourhan discussion). Yet even pure repetition requires some memory to follow what came before. Mark: From the humanist/Aristotelian mnemonic tradition, memory (retaining an impression), habit (pattern from repeated action), disposition/hexis (character from habituation), and recollection (actively searching memory) are distinct — but part of one larger system, "a single tree" with different branches. Kei agreed this may be why Peirce never fully equates habit with memory.
