SIGPSY Meeting: Pitrim Sorokin's Cyclical Theory of History

Pitrim Sorokin's cyclical theory of social and cultural dynamicsSensate, ideational, and idealistic phases of historical cyclesCritique of cyclical theories through Malthusian critique lensAppleby's 'Relentless Revolution' and its critique of cyclical thinking

Participants: _vgr

The SIGPSY group held a meeting focused on Pitirim Sorokin's "Social and Cultural Dynamics" and his influential cyclical theory of history. The theory posits that societies progress through recurring phases: sensate (emphasizing material and sensory experience), ideational (emphasizing spiritual and transcendent values), and idealistic (balancing both). The presenter provided multiple reference materials including the P2P Foundation wiki overview, PhilosophEasy article, and Wikipedia entry on Sorokin to ground the discussion.

Upon reflection, the presenter reconsidered his initial positive assessment of Sorokin's cyclical theory after engaging with Joyce Appleby's critique in "Relentless Revolution." The presenter noted that Appleby identifies a Malthusian hangover underlying cyclical theories of history, suggesting these frameworks carry pessimistic assumptions about human progress and resource limitations. This critique challenges the appeal of cyclical models that the presenter previously found compelling, indicating a shift toward questioning the theoretical underpinnings of cyclical versus linear models of historical change.

  • Sorokin's theory proposes that history moves through cyclical phases of sensate (material-focused), ideational (spiritual-focused), and idealistic (balanced) cultures.
  • The presenter initially found Sorokin's cyclical theory compelling but reconsidered it in light of Appleby's argument that cyclical theorists carried a Malthusian hangover in their thinking.
  • There is tension between cyclical and linear views of history, with modern critiques questioning the theoretical foundations of cyclical models.

Session Recording Summary · 48m 3s · Full notes ↗

Reading: The session discussed the cyclical theory of history of Pitirim Sorokin, focused on his model of alternating cultural "supersystems" — Sensate, Ideational, and Idealistic phases. The specific text was reading material Venkatesh (Venkat) shared in the chat; the exact title/chapter is not clearly named, though it draws on Sorokin's multi-volume Social and Cultural Dynamics (the group references its ~2,000+ page length).

The group read short materials on Sorokin's cyclical theory and then went around sharing reactions, mostly finding the cyclical mechanism unconvincing but the underlying observations and psychology compelling. Several participants proposed mapping Sorokin's ideas onto the group's own "world machines" framework, with recurring interest in what triggers transitions between phases. The discussion closed with a methodological reflection from Venkatesh on how to integrate many competing historical theories into a more robust model of their own.

  • Andre: Found the proposed cyclical mechanisms to lack a real "tool" or driver — compared them to astrological fate ("mister Bruno" and the stars controlling fate). Suggested the "world machines" model generates similar outcomes but more measurably and predictably. Offered human attention as a resource as an example: pre-modernity (e.g., idle winters after harvest) had attention in surplus; modernity and the "diversity machine" (social media) drew it down to a deficit. Prophesied that a future "liveness machine" would either use less human attention or expand our capacity to attend to more.
  • Florian: Felt somewhat put off because Sorokin's phase orientations seemed arbitrary — the tendencies feel like they exist simultaneously rather than sequentially. Found most interesting that in Sorokin's model, phase changes are triggered internally: a given direction reaches its limits and from within triggers a transition to a new paradigm. Noted this transition mechanism could be useful for the world machines model.
  • Sean: Found the cyclical aspect least compelling and struggled to identify the mechanism, but did not discard it entirely. Found it interesting to characterize the modern West as going through a Sensate period (traced from the Renaissance, growing affluence and materialism). Found Sorokin's characterization of ancient Rome as Sensate (contrasted with idealistic ancient Greece) persuasive, and was drawn to the High Middle Ages as an idealistic synthesis. Noted Sorokin's biography (escaped the Russian Revolution, nearly executed, founded Harvard's sociology department). Highlighted Sorokin's later work on altruism and love — mocked as insufficiently serious for mid-century American academia — as worth taking seriously, especially since "machines" (vs. cycles) allow for divergence, where different parts of the world could run altruism "experiments" in real time. Suspected Hegelian influence (thesis/synthesis).
  • Venkatesh: First read the material a decade ago and found it evocative/literary. Raised Joyce Appleby's skeptical critique (from The Relentless Revolution) that Toynbee and Spengler confuse Malthusian feast-famine cycles with genuinely historical dynamics — and suspected this partly applies to Sorokin, though ChatGPT suggested Sorokin partly accommodates the empiricist critique. Proposed a modified thesis: cultures/people seek agency in inner, idealistic/solipsistic worlds when they lack agency over the external world, and turn outward/materialistic when they gain external agency. Illustrated with Russia (volatile, hard-to-defend geography → idealistic culture of platonic forms; but oil/gas gave Putin's Russia outward material agency) and Saudi Arabia/Arabs (historically spiritual nomads at the mercy of harsh environment → materialistic after oil). Noted complications: Putin's regime shows renewed ideational tendencies (Byzantine-inheritor ideology, dominionist Christian movements), so the mapping isn't clean. Traced a recursive/pendulum dynamic in Britain: ideational High Middle Ages (honor/chivalry culture) swinging toward Sensate by the Scottish Enlightenment and steam engine era, with philosophers shifting from idealism (Hume) toward philosophy of science (Bertrand Russell). Credited Sorokin's psychological intuition (idealistic/ideational modes can exhaust themselves via diminishing metaphysical returns) but judged the causal mechanism not fully worked out — though "work-outable." Thought Sorokin was born too early (died ~1968, cybernetics just taking off) to put empirical meat on the theory.
