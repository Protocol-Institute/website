Toward a Mathematical Science of Protocols: Formalization and Agency

Formalizing protocols as constraints in possibility space and world tracesThe relationship between agency, nature, and ontological positioningDistinguishing protocols from related concepts (policies, correlated equilibria, emergent vs. engineered systems)The role of formal constitution in reifying protocols from informal to formal structuresMethodological challenges in balancing conceptual clarity with mathematical formalization

Participants: _vgr, _ergod, ediblebadger, mtraven

The SIGFPT group convened to review a proposed project on formalizing protocols through mathematical and ontological frameworks. The discussion centered on how to rigorously define protocols as computational constraints on possible world evolutions, drawing on world-trace approaches. A central tension emerged around the proper ontological placement of agency: participants debated whether agency should be treated as external to nature or embedded within it as a particular organizational structure. The group also grappled with scope questions—whether the formalization should accommodate only engineered protocols or extend to emergent, "soft" protocols found in nature and society.

Key methodological concerns included the need for worked examples to clarify which defined terms are conceptual versus formal, and skepticism about using supervenience relations to model emergence. Participants noted that protocols differ from other formal entities like nations or corporations, which typically become institutionalized through explicit constitutional acts, whereas protocols often lack such formal reification. The project lead acknowledged the work remains in early stages, focusing on building the mathematical framework and ontological architecture rather than applications, with plans to stress-test various components through collaborative reading and critique.

  • Protocols may be best understood as computable restrictions of world evolutions that create equivalence classes of futures through indifference sets, making intractable extrapolation tractable.
  • Agency should be modeled as embedded within nature through a particular asymmetric relationship involving observation and intervention, rather than as an external ontological layer imposed on top of nature.
  • The distinction between hard-engineered and soft, emergent protocols remains unresolved; the framework must clarify whether it applies only to formal protocols or can accommodate natural emergence like DNA and language.
  • Protocols differ from nations/corporations in that the latter typically undergo formal reification through constitutional acts, while protocols may exist without such formal institutionalization.
  • Supervenience relations for modeling emergence may be too strong; rhizomatic or entangled layer topologies might better capture how protocols emerge from world models.

Session Recording Summary · 89m 23s · Full notes ↗

Reading: A research program proposal authored by Ergod (presented in a Google Doc with an accompanying web page/slides). Its central question: "Can protocols become objects of mathematical science?" The proposal frames protocols as a "second-class" rather than "first-class" theoretical object, built up from an agent/nature base ontology. It connects prior Protocol Institute work — the phenomenological "protocol watching" tradition, the "unreasonable effectiveness/sufficiency of protocols" document, and the newer "new nature" framing.

The session was a first-pass review of Ergod's proposed mathematical research program on protocols. After brief reading time, participants did a relay round of reactions and questions, which Ergod answered in a consolidated block and then supplemented with a walkthrough of the program's "vertical slice" (agent/nature cut → fields → artifacts → technology → protocolization → new nature). The arc moved from broad enthusiasm for the "second-class" reframe, through clusters of probing questions (agent/nature distinction, representation of protocols, emergence, falsifiability, computational tractability), to closing logistics about the new alternating track structure and the November book-writing month.

  • Second-class vs. first-class framing (central move): Ergod argues protocols should not be assumed as fundamental primitives but distilled from world execution and trace analysis, and only later (potentially) rebuilt as a first-class theory. Several participants singled this out as the proposal's most compelling idea. Venkatesh: sees it as a powerful reframe that synthesizes two prior visions separated by a ~four-year gap; likes making the second-order ideas central — nature as fields, agents as capability profiles, protocols as grammars over world traces. Shingai: appreciated the contrast, noting he had worked top-down from first-class; Patrick and Mike also endorsed the direction.
  • Agent/nature distinction as a modeling choice: Ergod frames it via "situated asymmetry" — agents are part of nature and never violate natural law, but are capable of "agentic interruption" (lawful interventions in natural evolution that an external observer can distinguish from pure natural evolution).
  • Fields as the substrate: Originally inspired by sigma-fields (for agent information structures), Ergod shifted to fields in general as a flexible way to encode all "world content" — physical aspects plus abstract ones (resources, logistics, signaling/marker fields). The goal is a world model that can be executed, not a "sterile" formalism.
  • Protocols as grammars over traces: Presented explicitly as a hypothesis/hunch. Since "protocol watching" means analyzing traces generated by world execution, Ergod expects protocols to show up as a language/grammar recoverable via trace theory, multisets, etc.
  • The vertical slice (program structure): agent/nature cut (situated asymmetry) → nature as aggregation of fields evolving via laws → agents acting via policies with structured capability profiles → indirection principle (both observation and intervention are indirect; no direct writing to the environment) → conservation domains (coherence/invariance conditions) → world execution generating trajectories → persistent structural regularities → artifacts (regularities whose realization depends on agency) → technology (artifacts used to improve capability profiles) → protocolization (progressive hardening of an interaction grammar) → new nature (a sufficiently hardened regime).
  • Distinguishing law / policy / protocol: Ergod sharply separates three concepts: a law (inviolable, close to nature), a policy (what agents follow to organize behavior), and a protocol (violable — agents can deviate). Violability is key to the falsification method: if a trace-recovered structure can be violated in the space of possibilities, it's a protocol; if not, it's as strong as a law.

Questions & Disagreements: - Shingai: Worried the early stages will be useful for simulations/mathematical results but won't yet provide tools to compare, classify, and measure real-world protocols across domains. Ergod agreed that comparison/classification is deliberately deferred — you must first define/distill what a protocol is abstractly. - Patrick & Mike: Both pressed on the agent/nature distinction …
