SIGFPT Theory Track: Nature as Fields (Pilot Session)

Field theory as a unifying abstraction across physics (Maxwell), psychology (Lewin), and sociology (Bourdieu)Defining fields formally: typed fields with lawful evolution and observable regularitiesAgent/nature boundary as a stance rather than primitive; hardening of protocols through agent integrationTrust as a field: relational positions, visibility surfaces, and triadic coupling effectsPhase transitions and collective behavior: fluidization, stadium waves, and emergent protocols

Participants: _vgr, _ergod, ediblebadger, kylemathews, mtraven, shalimar0856, jdbb

The SIGFPT Theory Track's pilot session explored field theory as a foundational abstraction for modeling worlds at multiple scales. The group examined how Maxwell (electromagnetic fields in space), Lewin (psychological environment surrounding agents), and Bourdieu (social position in relational space) all use field concepts to explain how local configurations enable or constrain behavior. Kyle Mathews synthesized these readings by defining fields as the setup around something that determines what it can do next, with each theorist emphasizing different aspects: propagation through space, agent expectations, and social relations respectively.

A major theoretical move came from Mike Traven's proposal that the agent/nature boundary should be treated as a stance that can be moved, rather than a primitive distinction. This reframes protocols: they are patterns that become hardened when you can fold conforming agents into nature without changing the observable trace language. Shalimar developed this further by modeling trust as a field over relational positions and visibility surfaces, where interactions between two parties create cascading effects for observers—a form of triadic coupling. The group explored how collective phenomena like stadium waves and mob dynamics exhibit phase transitions from individual agents to fluid-like behaviors, suggesting fields naturally bridge micro and macro scales.

Participants noted the pilot session was ambitious for 25 minutes and discussed transitioning toward a research medium with LaTeX support and properties better suited to continuous artifact development. They plan to continue exploring these ideas in a follow-up session in four weeks, treating this as the opening of a longer theoretical inquiry into how protocols emerge from and operate within field dynamics.

  • A field can be defined as 'the setup around something that helps explain what it can do next,' with Maxwell (space between things), Lewin (psychological environment), and Bourdieu (social position) each offering different instantiations of this core concept.
  • The agent/nature cut should be treated as a movable stance: a protocol is fully hardened when you can fold conforming agents into nature without changing the trace language, making violability precisely what disappears in that folding.
  • Trust functions as a field over relational positions and visibility surfaces, where interactions between two parties propagate effects to observers through 'triadic visibility coupling,' causing public betrayal or repair to cascade across network topology.
  • Protocols can be understood as emergent patterns that form around existing field dynamics—like stadium waves—once agents adopt shared conventions to coordinate or modulate the underlying field behavior.
  • Collective behavior exhibits phase transitions from individual agents to fluid-like masses, suggesting fields provide a natural language for modeling both micro and macro dynamics.

Session Recording Summary · 68m 3s · Full notes ↗

Reading: This was the first session of a new "field theory of protocols" theory track. The reading consisted of several excerpts chosen by Ergod: a primary reading from James Clerk Maxwell (on fields), plus excerpts from Kurt Lewin (psychology/field theory — specifically cited pages ~25, 56–57) and Pierre Bourdieu (referred to phonetically as "Borjeo/Bordea/Bordeaux" — on fields of relations). Specific chapter titles were not clearly stated.

Ergod introduced the track, which aims to distill a workable (and maximally expressive) mathematical definition of a "field" as a mode of describing nature across scales, in service of eventually "constructing a world" for protocol/agent modeling. Participants did ~25 minutes of guided reading, then gave relay-style feedback. The discussion ranged over the agent/world distinction, distance effects, micro vs. macro scales, intersubjectivity, and field-like social phenomena, before Ergod closed by comparing the three authors and proposing Lewin's formulation as the weakest (and therefore most useful) starting definition.

  • Ergod (framing): The goal is to motivate intuition enough to extract a minimal mathematical definition of a field, then extend it in multiple directions — including to non-tangible/relational phenomena, not just physical ones. He introduced a "long-lived artifact" (shared document) to collect input beyond the session.
  • Ergod (author comparison): Maxwell introduced fields to eliminate Newtonian "action at a distance," repairing it with the concept of a medium through which information propagates. Bourdieu also uses "field" but drops the medium, modeling instead a field of relations — a more radical move that breaks Maxwell's founding intuition. Lewin (psychology) offers two statements: behavior derives from a totality of coexisting facts, and those facts form a dynamic field where each part depends on every other. Crucially, Lewin does not require continuity (allowing discrete fields) nor local propagation (everything affects everything simultaneously). Ergod judged this the weakest, most expressive definition — a good baseline the group can strengthen by later adding directionality/propagation/locality. Ergod highlighted Lewin's concept of boundary (life space) as essential to characterizing a field, teasing the eventual agent/nature connection.
  • Mike Travers: Philosophically resistant to a hard world/agent split, preferring to treat agents and world as the same substance; suggested treating the agent/nature cut as a stance rather than a primitive (reading from Claude: define an agent as any subsystem the modeler chooses to describe with observation/intervention maps plus a policy). Found the Lewin reading notably self-aware about its own formalism's limits.
  • Robby Rao: Liked that fields encapsulate distance effects — proximity to a source changes available possibilities (e.g., a government official vs. an ordinary citizen under a protocol).
  • Senthil Gandhi: Proposed modeling the economy as a field, noting market phenomena (e.g., Nvidia pricing correlating with Google TPU maturity) behave as if full information is already distributed — not point-to-point communication. Cautioned that in agent cases (a deer fleeing a predator) the relevant "space between" agents may be evolutionary memory, not the medium itself.
  • Shalimar: Focused on question five, exploring trust in a non-coercive, decentralized system and how it could be defined as a field; working with an agent to draft definitions to share in the thread.
