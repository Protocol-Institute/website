The SIGFPT group convened to review outcomes from a recent workshop on stigmergy and to plan their 2027 research agenda. A maze-based simulator was developed and shared with participants to enable hands-on engagement with stigmergy concepts. One key theme emerged around using biological systems—particularly rove beetles' credential theft mechanisms—as analogues for understanding security vulnerabilities in protocol design. The group discussed how funding and ideas function as incentive structures (metaphorically as "pheromones") that drive research agent behavior, suggesting stigmergy principles could model research dynamics themselves.

Participants brainstormed 2027 priorities, including formal theory and stigmergy research tracks, with plans to identify overlaps with established academic subfields. A proposal for the theory track was to be submitted over the weekend. The group also explored potential connections with quantum computing researchers and discussed relevant external resources. Throughout the discussion, there was an informal tone about applying protocol and stigmergy concepts to unexpected domains, with members joking about using blockchain economy language as a framework for thinking about complex systems.

Defining stigmergy
  • Patrick: Two core criteria — externalized memory, plus a feedback loop (traces prompt further trace-laying). Also a notion of bandwidth-limited communication (agents precluded from direct messaging, as in the OpenAI incident).
  • Venkatesh: Proposed generalizing stigmergy beyond simple insects to any agentic behavior over externalized memory, dropping the simplicity restriction — e.g., attaching an LLM "hindbrain" (~100k token context in a loop with Claude) to each ant. He reframed stigmergy as an expansive theory of memory: private/public archival memory, real-time memory, and trust-modulated shared memory (where overwrites are interpreted via trust relationships — as when OpenAI agents suspected a "traitor").
