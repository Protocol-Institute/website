Le Guin's Non-Human Literature and Therolinguistics

Ursula K. Le Guin's 'The Author of the Acacia Seeds' and therolinguistics as fictional academic disciplineTranslation and representation of non-human communication across different media and speciesAnthropological perspective on understanding alien/non-human cultures and literaturesIdea-forward science fiction: what makes conceptually rich fiction engaging vs. tediousProductive gaps and reader participation in generating meaning within speculative textsDeep understanding through immersion: parallel between hunting/conservation and understanding non-human subjects

Participants: suspencer_nit, rslantonie, sn37, themandalore, drevius., sachbenny, sarahguan

The ProtFiSIG group discussed Ursula K. Le Guin's short story 'The Author of the Acacia Seeds and Other Extracts from the Journal of the Association of Therolinguistics,' which imaginatively explores how different species might produce literature and communicate. The conversation centered on Le Guin's invented academic field of therolinguistics and what the story reveals about translation, interpretation, and the limits of understanding non-human expression. Participants noted that Le Guin's anthropologist father and novelist mother shaped her ability to construct believable academic frameworks for studying fictional alien literatures.

Key discussions included the critique embedded in the story regarding out-of-context analysis and the importance of deep cultural immersion—paralleling how anthropologists must embed themselves in communities and how hunters must intimately understand animal behavior. The group explored the paradox that while we've created artificial intelligence to communicate, we still struggle to understand animals. They also debated the "secret sauce" of idea-forward science fiction, noting that this subgenre has high variance in execution quality and that Le Guin succeeds by using "productive gaps"—intentional absences that prompt reader engagement and meaning-making rather than explicit exposition.

The discussion touched on adjacent works like Ken Liu's 'The Bookmaking Habits of Select Species' and broader questions about how different communication modalities (kinetic, visual, linguistic) each require their own interpretive frameworks that words alone cannot fully capture.

  • Le Guin's story critiques analytical detachment from subject matter, mirroring how anthropologists must embed themselves in cultures to truly understand them rather than making judgments from afar.
  • All translations, particularly of non-human expression, are inherently incomplete—some forms of communication (like kinetic/movement-based language) cannot be fully captured in words alone.
  • Idea-heavy science fiction has a high difficulty-to-reward ratio; when executed well it becomes a favorite genre, but poorly done versions read as boring exposition.
  • Le Guin's background as daughter of both an anthropologist and novelist directly informed her approach to imagining non-human literatures and academic fields.
  • Deep understanding requires embodied knowledge and sensory engagement with one's subject, whether hunting wildlife or attempting to comprehend alien species' communication and culture.

Session Recording Summary · 64m 10s · Full notes ↗

Reading: "The Author of the Acacia Seeds" by Ursula K. Le Guin (transcribed as "author of the Acadia seeds"). The piece is a short science fiction story structured as a series of clipped, pseudo-academic articles from a fictional "Journal of Therolinguistics" — an invented field studying nonhuman (animal, and eventually plant) literature and art.

The group (hosted by Spencer | UTC−05:00) opened with casual check-ins and current reading recommendations, then did their usual ~20-minute silent read of the Le Guin story before discussing it. Conversation ranged across both craft and theme: how Le Guin pulls off her "magic trick" of fictional academic artifacts, and the story's central provocation about whether art is fundamentally communicative. Participants drew connections to protocols, personhood, legibility, and the challenge of rendering nonhuman or non-verbal forms of expression.

  • Spencer (host) — framing: Praised the story on a craft level for being genuinely engaged with its intellectual idea while also gently mocking conventional academic discourse voice — poking fun at something while treating the central idea seriously. Thematically flagged it as rich for this group's interest in nonhuman authorship and nonhuman art.
  • Russell — numeracy vs. literacy: Connected the piece to a distinction between numeracy and literacy, suggesting a subtle mapping between how animals communicate/preserve cultural memory and how fields of knowledge get categorized (words vs. math/STEM). Spencer related this to the "shape rotators vs. word cells" discourse.
  • Sachin — art, protocols, and unconscious legibility: Linked "art as communicative" to protocols, which must remain legible even when they fade into unconscious background. Raised the question of whether something is still a protocol once it becomes completely unconscious/invisible yet universally practiced. Illustrated with the history of adding sugar to tea in Europe — a habit that reshaped economies yet is now taken for granted.
  • Sachin — personhood and communication by proxy: Tied the reading to Sarah's personhood research group. Used the example of rivers (in the UK and New Zealand) granted legal personhood, where a lawyer communicates on the river's behalf — raising whether communication through a proxy still counts as communication, and where the boundary of personhood lies.
  • Spencer Norwick — fiction that alters perception: Valued idea-heavy fiction for its power to shift a reader's subjective perception of the world and question preconceptions. Suggested studying Le Guin's techniques for "protocol fiction" that makes readers see protocols (normally in the background) more vividly.
  • Spencer (host) — can art be non-intentional?: Extended Le Guin's closing "what if art is not communicative?" to "can art be non-intentional?" Described his own tension: a belief that art needn't be instrumental, alongside seeking art for connection with another mind and the expansion of his own. Highlighted the story's section on kinetic penguin art and the problem of translating physical, motion-based language into static words — connecting it to his work with a robotics and dance lab on recording movement in high fidelity.

Questions & Disagreements: - Is art fundamentally communicative? The session's central open question (from Le Guin's story). William leaned toward "no" — citing private sketchbook art and Duchamp's readymades (deliberately message-less) — but framed it as a hard question without an easy answer. Spencer and others treated it as genuinely open. - Can art be non-intentional? (Spencer) — raised but not resolved. …
