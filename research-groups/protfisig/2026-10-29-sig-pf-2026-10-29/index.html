SIG-PF 2026-10-29

Participants: Sachin, Spencer, Chris Caine Lee, William Wickey

The group discussed the mutual influence between visual art and written fiction, moving from personal relationships to visual perception and art-making, through specific artists and movements (surrealism, cubism, Dada, impressionism), to the historical and technological contexts that shape how writers draw on images. A recurring thread was how AI image-diffusion models fit into this lineage. The conversation was exploratory and open-ended, with each participant sharing where the readings connected to their own practice and background.

  • Chris (opening): Raised aphantasia (the inability to form mental images on command) and his own atypical visual perception ("visual snow"/flickering static). He described skipping over lengthy scene descriptions in fiction (reading Iain Banks' Culture series) in favor of world rules, story structure, and dialogue—noting his theater and computational life-sciences background. He questioned whether writers can assume readers can even picture described scenes.
  • Chris on abstraction: Realistic, highly accurate painting leaves him emotionally cold; abstract, deconstructed, associative, and nonlinear work (cubism, collage, montage, expressionism) resonates more because he can "see the mind and emotion at work." He linked this to his perceptual experience of reality as already somewhat destabilized.
  • William: Found the readings timely after visiting the MoMA and seeing actual surrealist works (Ernst, Dalí, The Persistence of Memory). Argued the influence between visual art and fiction is deep and runs in both directions—partly because the mediums are so different that reinterpretation becomes generative. He noted (via Claude/ChatGPT research) that several celebrated 20th-century novelists explicitly credit visual artists, and that many wrote as art critics.
  • William on his own taste: Can picture things vividly and values rich visual detail and mood in prose (cited Cormac McCarthy). He also found the diffusion-model explainer conceptually fascinating—especially the idea of reversing the gradual deconstruction of images to generate new ones—since he works with AI-generated imagery.
  • Spencer: Grew up among architects, steeped in visual and structural art. Identified strongly with Woolf's idea that galleries can "evoke new words, new structures of language." Visual art unblocks his writing without the risk of pastiche that imitating admired writers creates, because there's no one-to-one translation.
  • Spencer on diffusion and theory: Noted that image models now generate via language prompting rather than visual-to-visual translation. He connected this to N. Katherine Hayles' How We Became Posthuman and her shift from a regime of "presence/absence" to one of "pattern/randomness," seeing diffusion models as a crisp example. He framed artistic techniques (e.g., free indirect discourse) as technologies, and wondered what new written techniques might emerge from a computational/abstract visual regime.
