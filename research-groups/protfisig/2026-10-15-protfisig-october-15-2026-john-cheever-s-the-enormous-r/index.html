ProtFiSIG October 15, 2026: John Cheever's 'The Enormous Radio'

John Cheever's 'The Enormous Radio' (1947) as proto-science fictionTechnology as intrusion into private life and its parallels to doomscrollingGenre analysis: how realist and science fiction approaches treat the same materialHistorical context of radio technology and post-WWII tech skepticismThe ending and themes of distraction from authentic meaning

Participants: suspencer_nit, jdbb, sn37, sachbenny, glorious_quail_86015, drevius.

The ProtFiSIG group discussed John Cheever's 1947 short story 'The Enormous Radio,' examining how it straddles literary realism and science fiction by introducing a supernatural technology into an otherwise realistic domestic setting. The story centers on a couple who discover their new radio can intercept their neighbors' private conversations, which the group found reminiscent of contemporary doomscrolling behavior and media consumption anxiety. Participants explored how the radio—a mature technology by 1947—becomes defamiliarized through its impossible function, creating a framework for discussing broader cultural anxieties about surveillance, privacy, and technological intrusion.

The discussion connected the story to post-WWII tech skepticism and traced a genealogy of similar concerns in later films exploring surveillance themes. Key thematic insights centered on how the characters become distracted and morally corrupted by their access to others' private lives, mistaking technological capability for deeper knowledge or fulfillment. The group concluded that the story's enduring power lies in its exploration of how people lose sight of authentic meaning and self-knowledge when seduced by technological novelty, with one participant noting that genuine self-understanding matters more than technical knowledge.

  • The story presents commonplace technology (radio) made strange through a sci-fi conceit, anticipating modern concerns about surveillance and involuntary information consumption similar to social media and doomscrolling.
  • The narrative evokes post-WWII tech skepticism and connects to a lineage of surveillance-focused cinema (Blow Up, The Conversation, Blow Out), suggesting persistent cultural anxieties about technology and observation.
  • The story's ending emphasizes how people become distracted by technological novelty and lose sight of authentic meaning and self-knowledge, with the implication that low-tech education and self-awareness are more valuable than understanding the machine itself.
  • Cheever's opening uses precise social positioning and class markers to establish the ordinariness of the protagonists, making the extraordinary incursion of the radio's capability more unsettling.
