Participants: Sarah Friend | utc +2, Mike Travers, Simon Hudson | utc-5, Sam Mandel (GMT -4), Anuraj, Ziva Ljubec, misha 🪨, Sachin, Eric Alston (UTC - 6), themandalore

The session explored Hacking's idea that classifications and categories "make up" new kinds of people through a looping feedback effect between labels and the people they describe. Discussion moved through examples (multiple personality disorder, schizophrenia, gender/sex on bureaucratic forms, occupation) and the roles of science, bureaucracy, politics, and capitalism in constructing personhood. The conversation then turned toward how these dynamics apply to AI — the deliberate construction of model "personas" — and closed with housekeeping and a proposal for a public-facing output from the group.

  • Mike: Hacking concerns the social construction of groups/types of people — a "macro" version of what Erving Goffman describes at the micro level (managing stigmatized personal identities). The two pair nicely.
  • Sam: The title "making up people" is catchy, but the LRB piece reads more about how science/social science defines key characteristics of personhood rather than personhood itself — you're still a person either way. He struggled to see how socially-shaped diagnoses amount to "manufacturing" people.
  • Sarah: Agreed the text is about categories/types of people more than personhood itself, but noted her own interest in how personhood was constructed as a lengthy historical evolution, and wondered whether that construction followed patterns similar to Hacking's.
  • Ziva: Emphasized Hacking's playful "dynamic nominalism" and the looping effect — people voluntarily "try on" new names and self-definitions (e.g. imagining parts of oneself in conflict), which expands the "play field" of selfhood. Quoted Nietzsche via Hacking: creating new names can create new things.
  • Sarah: Related this to earlier "persona/personage" discussions — e.g. identifying as an "artist" is a culturally available role that didn't exist in the same form centuries ago (closer to a portrait painter); such externally-named roles shape one's sense of self.
  • Sachin: The interesting core is categorization literally creating new types of people, not just roles. Examples: a family dealing with schizophrenia through religion rather than medicine when the diagnostic category is unavailable (via Rachel Aviv's Strangers to Ourselves); Macaulay's 1835 memo making English India's administrative language, shaping his own life generations later; the bureaucratic shift from "sex" to "gender" on forms. Argued bureaucracies create new people, often unintentionally, and that effects take generations to become invisible background.
