Participants: Ziva Ljubec, Sarah Friend | utc +2, misha 🪨, Eric Alston (UTC - 6)

The group discussed Riskin's history of attempts to artificially recreate life, tracing a line from entertainment automata through the industrial loom to present-day AI. Conversation centered on the shifting definition of what counts as "uniquely human," how technology devalues some human skills while opening new possibilities, and the commercial, political, and cultural power structures embedded in both historical automata and modern AI models. Discussion ranged across neuroscience feedback loops, labor and gender, language and colonialism, and model bias.

  • Ziva: The concept of "automaton" has inverted over time. In ancient thought (Aristotle, Greece) it meant self-arising, internal, self-moving force — essentially equivalent to life. The Enlightenment reframed it as an external mechanical simulation (e.g. the defecating duck), shifting it into a negative register: "it's just a machine, not organic."
  • Ziva: The essay's driving tension is the simultaneous impulse to simulate life and the conviction that it is impossible to simulate — these contradictory forces propel development. Sarah agreed, framing it as a "complicated relationship between the fantasy of simulating life and true intelligence always being situated in what is impossible to simulate."
  • Sarah: Favorite detail — the Mechanical Turk, though fraudulent, was historically useful. Babbage saw it and was inspired by its architectural separation of movement mechanisms from reasoning/control — a precursor to the now-familiar idea of abstracting software from hardware.
  • misha: The fakery of automata was partly driven by wishful thinking — they were "wished for," which is why frauds sometimes went undetected. He drew a parallel to current AI: statistically-driven "memory" still far from human intelligence, yet promoted as "superintelligence." Also noted AI apps that were actually routing work to human labor — the fraud persists.
  • misha: Naming matters — "Claude" sounds like a person (or a butler) and pushes a conversational, human-like boundary, whereas Alibaba's Qwen feels more like a tool, which he prefers.
  • Sarah: Riskin's point that we are "still having the same conversations." There is a feedback loop: building machines that imitate life teaches us about life (e.g. anatomy of a hand → modulating air pressure in lungs). This is alive today in the spiral between machine learning and neuroscience / computational linguistics.
