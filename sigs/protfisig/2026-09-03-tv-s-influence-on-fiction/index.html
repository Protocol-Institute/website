TV's Influence on Fiction

Television's formal influence on contemporary fiction writingIrony as a built-in literary technique across different media formsThe tension between earnestness and irony in modern literatureHow non-writing media (film, TV, video games) shape writers' approachesSocial media as a technomedia form and its literary implicationsDavid Foster Wallace's engagement with television and media criticism

Participants: sachbenny, suspencer_nit, sn37, jdbb, cameronisok, wwickey, drevius.

The ProtFiSIG group discussed David Foster Wallace's media criticism and television's influence on contemporary fiction writing. Participants examined Wallace's essay on TV through the lens of how television as a medium formally encodes irony—creating distance between signified and signifier—and how this shapes writers who come to fiction through visual media consumption. The discussion expanded to consider how different technomedia forms build in specific literary techniques: TV with irony, and social media with metonymy through algorithmic proximity and feed structure. A central debate emerged around whether earnestness and irony are fundamentally opposed or whether authentic earnestness requires holding ironic tension within a work, drawing on examples from Whitman, Kafka, and Borges. The group concluded that formal manipulation of bureaucratic or inert language, stretched to its limits, can paradoxically generate genuine earnestness through the accumulation of linguistic energy.

  • TV formally embeds irony through the distance between what's said and what's seen, influencing writers who consume primarily visual media rather than literature.
  • Social media is uniquely suited to metonymy as a literary technique, where meaning accrues from proximity and algorithmic juxtaposition of disparate thoughts and content.
  • Earnestness in fiction requires some degree of irony to be authentic; the tension between the two must be held within the work itself rather than resolved.
  • Formal engagement with inert, bureaucratic language and forms can generate genuine earnestness through accumulated linguistic energy, offering a path beyond the irony-sincerity binary.

The session began with informal chat (the concept of the "hipster" archetype being remapped onto the millennial generation) before a 20-minute silent reading period. The group then discussed DFW's essay on television's relationship to irony and fiction, extending its arguments to today's media environment — social media, infinite feeds, short-form video, and generative AI. Much of the conversation explored whether irony is "dead," how the medium shapes the kind of person it creates, and whether earnest/sincere expression is still possible in a hyper-observed, AI-saturated culture.

  • **Signal compression and irony as information density (Andre & Cameron):** Andre proposed that media forms are being "compressed" — TV added pictures to radio, and irony lets two data streams (text and contradicting subtext) reach the viewer at once. He wondered whether "non-humorous irony" could become a fertile path for information density rather than just a screen-glue feature. Cameron built on this using electromagnetic wave bands as a metaphor for attention: double/triple entendres "stuff more into the signal channel," and there's an attention limit — five layers of irony crowd out other elements like visual framing.
  • **Modern consumption as parallel/fragmented (Cameron):** He compared consuming many micro-clips to working on five parts of a project with an LLM simultaneously — "letting the machine run" — which functionally changes how you interact with the medium.
  • **DFW's prescience (Cameron):** He read the essay with "slow dawning horror," calling it an "incredibly well called shot." The nineties-era details (telecomputers, etc.) miss specifics, but the vibe felt spot-on. He highlighted DFW's point about the medium capturing the rhetorical device of irony.
  • **Shitposting as capitulation (Cameron):** Framed shitposting as an attempt to rebel against "the tidal wave of shit" you consume by re-embracing it — but embracing it is also capitulation, creating a "tar pit" where earnest storytelling is hard because you need a viewership that understands earnestness.
  • **New Sincerity / irony as poison (Sachin):** Sachin recalled a "new sincerity" moment where irony was treated as poisonous, requiring creators to sincerely believe their stories. On rereading, DFW's point about "image fiction" — combining and juxtaposing images — stood out. He noted TV's culture problems being addressed by dismantling images into recombinable chunks.
  • **Irony isn't dead, it's dissolved (Sachin):** Sachin argued irony didn't die but became part of reality so thoroughly it's no longer noticeable as irony; it "finds people who are trying to be sincere."

Questions & Disagreements: - **Cameron's open question:** Is earnestness even possible when you're aware you're trying to be earnest? He argued a writer can't truly journal earnestly because entries could be published posthumously; the assumption of privacy has been "obliterated," and AI chats are similarly non-private (legal discovery, OpenAI staff).
