    Participants: sachbenny, wwickey, suspencer_nit

    The Protocol Fiction SIG held its 13th and final session before a month-long break, examining the Deadspin article 'How Wile E. Coyote Explains the World' as a case study in protocol comedy. The group discussed how the Looney Tunes character and director Chuck Jones's work serve as a lens for understanding reality and meaning-making. Central to the discussion was the concept that physics in simulated or imagined spaces operate differently than in reality—governed by computational demands and conceptual constraints rather than traditional laws.

    Participants debated whether the rules extracted from Wile E. Coyote cartoons are fundamental to their comedic structure or rather post-hoc observations useful for sequential storytelling. They explored the distinction between the 'planning/rules' needed to sustain multiple episodes versus those that generate initial humor. The group also connected this discussion to narrative rhythm and the Kuleshov effect, examining how story physics emerge from the properties of different beat sequences (2, 3, or 4 beats) rather than individual scenes. References to computational limits, escape from designed realities, and the detailed texture of actual reality enriched the theoretical framework.

    • Laws of physics in simulated spaces reduce to compute/power laws—more imaginative acts demand more computational resources, creating a conceptual gravity that limits change and exploration in imagined realities.
    • The 'rules' identified in the Wile E. Coyote article are largely post-hoc constructions useful for sustaining multiple episodes, rather than the foundational rules that make the first episode funny.
    • Planning is important while plans are useless—this paradox applies to narrative rules and structures, suggesting rules emerge from necessity rather than precede creation.
    • Story physics operate on properties of narrative beat sequences (2 vs. 3 vs. 4 beats), revealing rhythm as a key element distinct from individual scene physics.

Sachbenny introduced Adair's essays and fiction samples, emphasizing that Adair's entire practice constituted sophisticated 'style transfer'—pastiche, parody, and adaptation of other writers' work. The group discussed how conceptual and technical constraints in Adair's writing (particularly in translation work like 'A void') could serve as a model for distinguishing quality prose from the undifferentiated output of language models. The conversation touched on Italo Calvino's Castle of Crossed Destinies as a related example of constraint-driven narrative experimentation, where tarot grid structures generate multiple interconnected stories.     • Gilbert Adair's entire career exemplified 'style transfer'—appropriating other writers' techniques across plot, character, metaphor, and diction—making him a fitting reference point for understanding LLM-generated prose.
    • Formal constraints, as demonstrated in Adair's translation work, can cut through the 'banal lustre' of language models by forcing deliberate stylistic choices that reveal craftsmanship.
    • Calvino's Castle of Crossed Destinies demonstrates ambitious constraint-based narrative experimentation through its tarot grid structure, exemplifying protocol-adjacent writing practices.
    • The distinction between masterful, laboriously-executed constrained writing and unconstrained generated prose hinges on demonstrable taste and hard work, raising questions about cultural value assessment.

    Compressed Epics: Protocol Fiction and Temporal Narrative
    Definition and characteristics of compressed epics as a narrative formProtocol fiction applications and how protocols evolve over centuriesHyperdiegetic storytelling and implied narrative depthUsing hyperobjects as anchors for compressed epic narrativesLe Guin's Ansible as a model for narrative gravity in compressed epics

    Participants: sachbenny, suspencer_nit, randylubin, thewanderingeditor

    The ProtFiSIG group explored compressed epics—a narrative form that spans only days or weeks while drawing in centuries of historical context—using examples like Pynchon's The Crying of Lot 49 and Borges' The Secret Miracle. The core appeal is creating a reading experience where vast temporal spans collapse into a single moment of perception. The group examined how protocols themselves function as ideal subject matter for compressed epics, since they evolve invisibly over centuries before becoming background infrastructure. They also discussed hyperdiegetic storytelling, which implies vast untold narratives through minimal textual references (like the Budapest reference in M.A.S.H.), and how this relates to the compressed epic form.

    A key theoretical contribution emerged around the concept of 'narrative gravity'—drawing an analogy to Le Guin's Ansible, where a massive object creates a gravitational center that naturally pulls other elements into orbit. The group proposed that hyperobjects (massive, persistent entities across time) could serve as such anchors in compressed epic fiction, naturally drawing historical and temporal concerns into the narrative without explicit exposition. They concluded that protocol fiction is particularly well-positioned to explore hyperobjects and deep-time narratives, as protocols are inherently systems that accumulate meaning and persistence across centuries.

    • Compressed epics compress vast historical timescales (hundreds of years) into brief narrative moments (days/weeks), creating the reader experience of 'seeing all of eternity in one instant'
    • Protocols naturally function as non-great-man historical themes that have evolved invisibly over centuries and can serve as the central conflict in compressed epic fiction
    • Hyperobjects—massive, persistent narrative anchors—may function similarly to Le Guin's Ansible principle, bending narrative gravity so that history naturally orbits the central temporal narrative
    • Hyperdiegetic storytelling (implying vast backstory through minimal textual cues) shares structural principles with compressed epics in suggesting depth beyond what is explicitly shown
    • Protocol fiction is uniquely situated to explore hyperobjects by examining how systems and protocols accumulate meaning and persistence across deep time

    Protocol Fairy Tales: Le Guin's Omelas and Fairy Tale Conventions
    Fairy tale formal elements (flatness, abstraction, intuitive logic, normalized magic) in protocol fictionLe Guin's 'The Ones Who Walk Away from Omelas' as a case study in narrative formDistinction between thought experiments and emotionally-charged storiesUse of generalized/archetypal characters versus psychological depth in speculative fictionMeta-narrative techniques and narrator anxiety in storytellingVagueness versus explanation in protocol fiction worldbuilding

    Participants: suspencer_nit, drevius., randylubin, wwickey, sachbenny

    The ProtFiSIG group analyzed Ursula K. Le Guin's 'The Ones Who Walk Away from Omelas' through the lens of Bernheimer's formal elements of fairy tales: flatness, abstraction, intuitive logic, and normalized magic. The discussion revealed that Le Guin uses archetypal, unnamed characters and abstract descriptions to create emotional impact, while incorporating meta-narrative elements where the narrator directly engages the reader about their ability to understand Omelas. This hybrid approach complicates the story's categorization—it functions simultaneously as both fairy tale and philosophical thought experiment.

    A key distinction emerged between thought experiments and emotional narratives: while a trolley-problem framing of Omelas might lead to logical analysis, Le Guin's story form generates emotional response and moral weight. The group noted that Le Guin's narrative anxiety—the narrator's concern with their own descriptive powers—mirrors writers' hesitations about how much to explain or leave mysterious. For protocol fiction specifically, participants suggested that strategic vagueness about systems and protocols yields greater impact than exhaustive technical explanation.

    The session concluded with open questions about whether protocol fiction can operate with dream logic or absurdist principles rather than consistent internal logic, and deeper exploration of how generalized characters serve narrative versus explanatory functions in speculative worldbuilding.

    • Le Guin employs fairy tale conventions by using unnamed, archetypal characters rather than psychologically complex individuals, creating abstraction that paradoxically makes the moral dilemma more emotionally resonant than a logical thought experiment would.
    • The meta-narrative elements in Omelas—where the narrator directly addresses the reader and acknowledges the writing process—create tension between the story's fairy tale form and its philosophical weight.
    • Stories differ from thought experiments through emotional charge: presenting Omelas as a trolley problem versus as a narrative produces different reader responses and conclusions.
    • Protocol fiction benefits from strategic vagueness about technical details and systems rather than exhaustive explanation, mirroring how fairy tales leave gaps that readers must intuitively fill.
    • The possibility of absurdist protocol fiction—operating without conventional logic—is raised as an open question for the group to explore.

Members analyzed a serialized story, appreciating its use of second-person perspective and evocative technical details, but noted that the continuous format became fatiguing and could be improved through condensing by approximately 30% and spacing releases. The meeting concluded with positive feedback about the story selection and emerging interest in developing asynchronous participation options to accommodate members unable to attend live sessions.

    • Network Realism is defined as fiction set in the near future with networked components that samples real-world artifacts, creating a 1:1 relationship between fiction and present reality.
    • Participants found serialized fiction formats engaging but potentially fatiguing, with suggestions that condensing content by 30% and spacing releases over 10 days could improve reader experience.
    • The genre faces an inherent challenge: as demonstrated by Charlie Stross's experience, rapidly advancing technology can quickly overtake near-future narrative predictions, making the work feel dated.
    • Deliberate vagueness and empty space in Network Realism stories function effectively to allow readers to fill gaps with contemporary context, extending the work's relevance.
    • Science fiction fundamentally operates as present-day social commentary rather than literal future prediction, which is central to understanding Network Realism's purpose and appeal.

    ProtFiSIG - Narrative Rapidity in Protocol Fiction
    Narrative rapidity techniques in protocol fictionStructural approaches to rapid fictionLinguistic tools for creating narrative speedBalancing parataxis and hypotaxis in rapid narrativesShowing protocol impacts versus explaining protocols in depth

    Participants: suspencer_nit, randylubin, timber1997, bah.eth, sachbenny, thewanderingeditor, drevius., mercerlain

    The ProtFiSIG group convened to discuss narrative rapidity—the quality that makes fiction read quickly—examining how this technique applies to protocol fiction. Members analyzed two primary texts: Calvino's discussion of Candide and a César Aira story, using these to identify structural and linguistic patterns. Key discussions revealed that rapidity in protocol fiction benefits from showing protocol impacts rather than explaining mechanics, and that linguistic choices (sentence structure, vocabulary selection) significantly influence reading speed. The group explored how multiple characters interacting with the same protocol, callback motifs, and careful balance between simple and complex sentence construction all contribute to rapid narrative movement.

    Participants identified specific techniques including the use of short phrases created through punctuation within longer sentences, preference for Saxon over Latinate words, and the strategic use of callbacks to earlier elements. The group also noted that rapidity isn't solely dependent on events; instead, structural elements like character interactions with protocols and thematic repetition can maintain momentum. Comparisons were drawn to successful rapid narratives like thriller writing and examples from Vonnegut, with the consensus that understanding the tension between parataxis and hypotaxis is fundamental to crafting rapid fiction.

    • Rapid narrative in protocol fiction works better when it shows the impacts and interfaces of a protocol rather than explaining it in depth, particularly in short-form work.
    • Narrative rapidity can be achieved through linguistic choices: simple word selection, Saxon over Latinate vocabulary, and breaking longer sentences into shorter phrases through punctuation.
    • Multiple characters encountering the same protocol from different angles creates structural rapidity without relying solely on events, preventing the story from becoming a mere sequence of occurrences.
    • Parataxis (simple conjunctions) and hypotaxis (complex subordination) exist in tension within rapid fiction, and mastering this balance is key to achieving reading velocity.
    • Rapid fiction uses callbacks and repeated motifs (like Candide's optimism philosophy) as structural devices to maintain coherence while moving quickly through events.

    Protocol Monsters: Cultural Technologies and Protocol Fiction
    Monsters as cultural technologies that embody societal fears and expose category instabilityWhen protocols become monstrous: emergence, uncontrollability, and lack of defensive measuresClassic monster stories with built-in defeat protocols versus Lovecraftian horror's absence of solutionsProtocol parasites and remoras: distinguishing between protocols that enable versus curb monstrousnessReal-world examples: work-to-rule as summoning demons, Flash Boys as cyberculture monsters, Protocols of the Elders of ZionBenevolent compliance and subversion as alternatives to malicious compliance in protocol fiction

    Participants: sachbenny, suspencer_nit, timber1997, randylubin

    The ProtFiSIG group explored the intersection of monsters and protocols in fiction, discussing how monsters function as cultural technologies that expose the instability of categorical boundaries. Drawing from an academic essay on monsters as embodiments of societal fears, the group examined when protocols themselves become monstrous—identifying emergence as a key characteristic that makes protocols horrific when they escape human control and contain no built-in defensive measures. The discussion evolved from classic monster narratives (which typically include protocols for defeat) to Lovecraftian horror (which deliberately excludes such safeguards), suggesting that the absence of a defensive protocol is itself the horror.

    Participants analyzed real-world and fictional examples including work-to-rule as protocol summoning, Flash Boys as cyberculture monsters, and the Gordeon knot as a metaphor for protocols that can only be destroyed, not reformed. They introduced a distinction between protocols that enable monstrousness versus those that prevent it, using biological metaphors of parasites and remoras. The group also explored the potential for human agents to be weaponized by protocols, positioning them as monsters to others, with possibilities for reclaiming humanity through subversion or what some called "benevolent compliance"—a counterpoint to malicious compliance.

    Key tensions emerged around protocol safety: while increased safety mechanisms reduce classical kinetic failure modes that previously provided narrative fodder, they also create new horrors when protocols become uncontrollable. The group suggested that understanding monsters through protocol fiction helps illuminate how systems can escape their designers' intentions and how individuals might resist or subvert protocols that attempt to conscript them into monstrous roles.

    • Monsters embody a society's anxieties about difference and power by refusing stable classification, making them useful metaphors for understanding how protocols police boundaries and expose their instability.
    • A protocol becomes monstrous when it exhibits emergence properties that place it beyond individual or collective control, as exemplified by Lovecraft's horrors which lack any defensive protocol—the real horror is having no means of protection.
    • Classic monster stories (silver bullets, garlic, crosses) represent embedded defensive protocols, whereas modern protocol-based horrors like Lovecraftian entities deliberately deny such escape routes, fundamentally changing the narrative structure.
    • Protocols can be reimagined through the lens of parasitism (harmful) versus symbiosis (beneficial), with work-to-rule representing a kind of summoning of monsters through protocol subversion.
    • Human agents can be positioned as monsters by protocols that conscript them to fulfill inhuman wishes, with potential for reclaiming agency through subversion or benevolent compliance rather than malicious compliance.

    • Cosmic horror's most effective endings place protagonists at an abyss's edge—either sinking into it or escaping traumatized and forever changed—rather than providing complete resolution.

    The ProtFiSIG group convened to discuss genre's utility, specifically exploring the concept of genre as a contract between authors and readers. The discussion examined how genre communicates expectations about world-building, character development, plot coherence, and narrative logic. Members analyzed how violation of these contracts—such as No Country for Old Men subverting western genre conventions—can either enhance or alienate readers depending on their genre literacy and expectations. The group debated how strictly genres must adhere to their promises, using examples like John Dickson Carr's The Burning Court (which introduced magical elements after a mystery solution) and the Harry Potter series (which uses mystery as a gateway into fantasy). Participants explored the paradox of unreliable narrators needing absolute factual reliability within their constraints, and discussed how late-stage genre development risks becoming over-optimized audience capture.

    • Genre functions as a contract that sets reader expectations about world-building, character, plot logic, and probability, with violations occurring when authors deliberately break these established promises.
    • Unreliable narrators present a paradox: they must be MORE reliable than standard narrators in narrow senses (cannot lie outright) even though they aren't on the reader's side, maintaining the fairness of the contract.
    • Late-stage genre development risks becoming 'audience capture,' where works are over-engineered through excessive feedback loops, losing the vital balance of being 'surprising yet inevitable.'
    • Genre can be understood as Delany's 'subjunctive modes'—how works relate to reality—and theme may be an emergent property of well-rendered setting rather than a separate element.

    ProtFiSIG Pilot Meeting: Protocol Fiction Definitions and Genre Analysis
    Defining protocol fiction vs. special person fiction through Die Hard and Lord of the Rings analysisThe distinction between characters who are inherently superpowered versus systems that treat characters speciallyGolden age detective fiction rules (Knox's rules) as a model for protocol fiction rule-buildingLiterary constraint traditions (Oulipo, Fluxus, Cut-up) as approaches to protocol fictionProtocol romance as a genre variant with embedded system criticism

    Participants: suspencer_nit, _vgr, sachbenny, timber1997, maertia, amitashu, thewanderingeditor, mercerlain, jdbb, drevius., kannen123

    The ProtFiSIG pilot meeting focused on defining protocol fiction and distinguishing it from special person fiction through detailed textual analysis. The group examined key examples like Die Hard and Lord of the Rings, debating whether characters were inherently special or simply operating within systems that treated them specially. A critical insight emerged: original Die Hard works as protocol fiction because McClane is replaceable, but sequels converted him into a special person archetype, diminishing the protocol fiction quality. The discussion traced historical precedent to golden age detective fiction (Knox's rules), noting that protocol fiction inverts typical genre development by starting with rules and iterating rather than deriving rules from existing works. The group then explored constraint-based literary traditions (Oulipo, Fluxus, Cut-up, J.G. Ballard) as potential methodological frameworks for developing protocol fiction. The conversation concluded by expanding the conversation toward genre variants like protocol romance, emphasizing that criticism of the protocol itself may be more fruitful than elaborating rules.

    • Protocol fiction is unusual in starting with rules and then testing/iterating them, inverting the typical genre development where rules are descriptive of existing works.
    • Characters can be 'special' in two distinct ways: through inherent superpowers or through systems bending around them (e.g., Frodo's ring immunity versus Superman's powers), and distinguishing between these is crucial to understanding protocol vs. special person fiction.
    • The original Die Hard functions as protocol fiction because John McLane is interchangeable—he could be replaced by any competent cop in that system—but sequels transformed him into a special person, losing the protocol fiction quality.
    • Constraint-based literary movements (Oulipo, Fluxus, Cut-up, Ballard) offer methodological parallels for developing and exploring protocol fiction rules and narrative possibilities.
    • Protocol romance and other genre variants may be better understood through criticism of the protocol itself rather than elaboration of its rules.
