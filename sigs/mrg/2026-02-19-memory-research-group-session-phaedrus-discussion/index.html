Memory Research Group Session - Phaedrus Discussion

Platonic theory of anamnesis and learning as soul's recollectionPharmakon concept: writing as remedy and poison for memoryDistinction between memoria (genuine internal memory) and reminiscentia (externally-prompted recollection)Thamus's critique of writing as false cure for memoryAnalysis of Phaedrus dialogue and classical memory philosophy

Participants: keikreutler, sachbenny, suspencer_nit, modefier

The Memory Research Group convened to discuss Plato's Phaedrus dialogue, establishing foundational concepts for their analysis. The group created a shared glossary defining key terms including anamnesis (Plato's theory of learning as recollection of pre-birth knowledge of divine forms), pharmakon (the dual nature of writing as both remedy and poison), and the crucial distinction between memoria and reminiscentia (internal memory versus externally-prompted recollection).

The central theme examined was Thamus's philosophical critique of writing: while Theuth presents writing as a pharmakon that could remedy memory problems, Thamus argues it is actually a poison disguised as a cure. According to this critique, writing creates the false appearance of memory retention while actually supporting only reminiscentia—the weaker act of being reminded through external reference—rather than developing genuine internal memoria. This distinction forms the core of the group's investigation into how external tools and technologies affect authentic memory and learning.

  • Anamnesis represents Plato's theory that learning is not acquisition of new knowledge but the soul's recovery of pre-existing knowledge from its vision of divine forms before birth.
  • Writing functions as a pharmakon—simultaneously a remedy and poison—with Thamus arguing that what appears to be a cure for memory actually weakens genuine internal memory retention.
  • The group distinguished between memoria (true, internally-stored knowledge) and reminiscentia (recollection dependent on external aids), with Thamus contending that writing only supports the latter, inferior form of memory.
