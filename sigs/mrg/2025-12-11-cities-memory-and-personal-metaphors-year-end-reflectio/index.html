Cities, Memory, and Personal Metaphors: Year-End Reflection

Italo Calvino's 'Invisible Cities' and literary representations of memoryMemory reconsolidation and how present frames reshape rememberingSpatial memory and embodied knowledge beyond visual recallMetaphors for memory: filing cabinets, clouds, and physical sequencesCollective vs. personal memory narratives and their evolution

Participants: keikreutler, thewanderingeditor, ruthvik8193

The Memory Research Group concluded their year by examining Italo Calvino's 'Invisible Cities,' focusing on how cities function as vessels for memory and meaning. The group engaged with excerpts that explore the relationship between spatial imagination and personal remembering. One participant noted they hadn't previously engaged with Calvino but were encouraged to explore the full text for its rich urban descriptions.

The discussion moved beyond literary analysis to explore the mechanics of memory itself. Participants shared that remembering is not a retrieval of fixed information but a continuous reconsolidation shaped by present concerns—evident in how collective narratives shift with contemporary contexts and how personal life stories are perpetually reshaped. The group examined embodied memory particularly, discussing how knowledge can be accessed through physical engagement and spatial placement rather than visual recall, using examples like learning music through inevitable sequences and yoga as physical knowledge transfer.

Several evocative metaphors emerged to describe memory's nature: a filing cabinet where knowledge is withdrawn from organized space without consciously seeing its contents, clouds that momentarily take solid forms revealing what currently occupies one's mind, and physical sequences that flow inevitably. These metaphors suggest memory operates across multiple modes—visual, spatial, embodied, and intuitive—with the caveat that explicit investigation of these mechanisms can paradoxically disrupt their function.

  • Every act of remembering reconsolidates memory through present-day frames, operating at both personal scales (reshaping life stories) and collective scales (shifting nation-state narratives with contemporary concerns).
  • Memory can function through embodied practice and spatial placement rather than visual imagery, as demonstrated by learning music through physical sequences despite aphantasia, though explicit investigation risks disrupting the mechanism.
  • Memory operates through multiple metaphorical systems—filing cabinets for organized retrieval without conscious seeing, clouds for momentary solidification revealing present concerns, and physical spaces for inevitable sequences.
  • Knowledge can be made available through embodied practice and physical engagement rather than mental retrieval, exemplified by yoga teaching and musical learning.
  • Investigating memory mechanisms too closely can potentially disrupt their function, suggesting the importance of tacit and intuitive knowledge.
