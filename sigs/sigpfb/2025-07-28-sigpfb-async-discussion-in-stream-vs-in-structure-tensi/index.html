SIGPfB Async Discussion: In-Stream vs. In-Structure Tension Management

Distinction between in-stream and in-structure protocols for managing organizational tensionsHow tension management approaches differ in crypto/blockchain contexts due to rigid permission structuresThe role of informal practices (coaching, apprenticeships) versus formal documented proceduresContinuous vs. discrete enactment of protocols and their evolution patternsCategory failure risks when implementing technologies (like LLMs) as structures instead of tools

Participants: timber1997, _vgr, rafa_0x, plague_year

The SIGPfB group held an async discussion on a preprint paper about managing tensions in business protocols. Due to quorum issues, participants engaged asynchronously with two guiding prompts about why certain tensions are managed in-stream versus in-structure, and risks of managing tensions in the wrong medium.

The conversation revealed a key distinction: in-structure protocols (formal rules, role specifications, office layouts) evolve through step-changes but have continuous effects, while in-stream protocols (coaching, apprenticeships, tactical maneuvering) evolve incrementally and activate through discrete individual interactions. Participants noted that in-stream practices, though often undocumented, are surprisingly critical to organizational success.

A particularly relevant insight emerged regarding crypto/blockchain contexts, where rigid permission structures around formal changes force reliance on in-stream management. The group concluded with a warning about category failures—attempting to implement in-stream technologies like LLMs as fixed structures rather than discretionary tools is likely to fail.

  • In-structure protocols evolve in step-changes but have continuous effects, while in-stream protocols evolve incrementally and are enacted discretely based on individual interactions.
  • In-stream tension management (coaching, apprenticeships) is often invisible in documentation but critically valuable for network deepening, whereas in-structure expertise proves valuable for cross-network transitions.
  • Crypto's rigid permission model for structural changes forces greater reliance on tactical in-stream maneuvering, making informal management practices unusually important.
  • Attempting to implement in-stream technologies (like LLM chatbots) as rigid structures is a category failure that likely dooms adoption; they work better as discretionary tools.
